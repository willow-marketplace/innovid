# Client letter guidelines

**Read this in full before drafting a single line of client-facing text.** The internal pack is for the advisor; this letter goes to the client, in the firm's voice, about their money. It is the highest-risk output in the library.

The letter is always **a draft**. Page one opens with:

> *Draft for advisor and compliance review — not sent.*

That line stays until a human removes it. This skill never sends anything.

---

## What the letter is

A warm, plain-language account of the year in the client's own story: what was done, where their goals stand, what changed for them, what happens next. Roughly 400–700 words. Four sections, in this order, using these headings or the advisor's preferred equivalents:

1. **What we did** — the decisions taken together, in plain language, in the order they happened. Actions with outcomes, not activity. "We consolidated the two old retirement accounts" — not "we reviewed your retirement accounts."
2. **Where your goals stand** — each goal, where it stood at the start of the window, where it stands now, and what moved it. Movement in either direction is stated honestly; a goal that pushed out is described as a decision they made, not a failure that happened to them.
3. **What changed** — the life events they shared, acknowledged with warmth and without embellishment. A retirement, a grandchild, a house move, a loss. One line each.
4. **What's next** — the open items and the agenda for the coming period. Concrete, small in number, and every one of them something the advisor has actually committed to.

Close with a single sentence in the advisor's voice, then the disclaimer block (see below).

## Tone — the advisor picks it, and it changes the register only

The advisor is asked which of four tones to write in before a word is drafted. **Tone changes how the letter sounds. It never changes which facts appear, what is redacted, or what is claimed.** A friendly letter is not a looser letter; a brief one carries the same disclaimer block. Everything under "Always, whatever the tone" below is non-negotiable in all four.

| Tone | Register | Opening, same facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Warm** (default) | Personal without being casual. First names, acknowledges the year they've had, reads as though someone who knows them wrote it. | "Peter, Paula — it's been a year of real change for you both, and I wanted to set down where things stand." |
| **Professional** | Measured and precise, closer to a formal client communication. Surnames or full names, no contractions, restrained. | "Dear Mr and Mrs Reynolds — this letter summarises the past twelve months and the position as it now stands." |
| **Friendly** | Conversational and light. Contractions, short sentences, the register of someone who enjoys these people. | "Peter, Paula — quite a year! Here's where we've got to and what I think comes next." |
| **Brief and factual** | The shortest defensible letter. What happened, where things stand, what's next. No scene-setting. | "Peter and Paula — a summary of the year to August 2026." |

Ask every run. Don't remember the choice: it can differ by client within the same firm, and the client who wants brevity is rarely the one who wants warmth.

### Always, whatever the tone

- **Second person.** "You decided", "we agreed", "you mentioned".
- **Plain English.** No "leverage", "utilize", "vehicles", "solutions", "space". If a sentence would sound strange read aloud at a kitchen table, rewrite it — including in the professional tone, which is reserved, not corporate.
- **Specific over effusive.** "You told us in March that the business sale had slipped to next spring" is warm because it shows they were heard. "We value our partnership with you" is filler and reads as a template. This is what makes the warm tone work; without it, warmth becomes padding.
- **No hedging stacks.** One clear statement beats three qualified ones.
- **Their language where it's on the record.** If the client called it "the Texas Tech money", the letter may call it that — drawn from the structured payloads, which is the only source available.
- **No emotional interpretation.** Report what they said and did. Never characterise how they felt — "we know this was a difficult year for you" is a claim about a person's inner life that they did not authorise and may not share. This holds hardest in the warm and friendly tones, where the pull toward it is strongest.
- **No invented detail.** A tone is a way of writing the facts, never a licence to add one. If a friendlier sentence needs a fact that isn't on the record, write a plainer sentence.

## Redaction — default off unless explicitly enabled

| Content | Rule |
|---|---|
| Account balances, portfolio values, any figure | **Excluded** unless the advisor asks for figures in this letter. Even then: only figures the client stated or was formally shown, never derived or estimated |
| Performance, returns, growth | **Never.** Not even factually, not even favourably. Performance communication is a regulated act with format requirements this skill cannot satisfy |
| Internal metrics of any kind (coverage counts, rankings, labels) | **Never.** These grade the advisor and mean nothing to the client |
| Comparison to other clients, to the firm, to "clients like you" | **Never** |
| Third-party names — children, parents, professionals | **Excluded** by default. "Your family" not "Sarah and Michael". A named non-client in a letter is a person who never consented to being written about |
| Health information | **Never**, including the client's own, unless the advisor explicitly overrides for this one letter and it is central to a decision that was made ("following your retirement on health grounds") |
| Estrangements, family conflict, divorce detail | **Never** |
| Anything not on the record from a real meeting | **Never.** The letter may only contain what the structured record supports |

When redaction removes something load-bearing, leave the gap and tell the advisor in the handoff note — don't paper over it with vaguer prose that implies more than was said.

---

## Prohibited content — hard list

Never appears in a client letter under any framing, request, or reassurance that the firm allows it:

- Forward-looking assurance of any kind: "you're on track", "you'll be fine", "this should get you there", "we expect".
- Projections, targets presented as likely outcomes, or probability language.
- Performance figures, returns, benchmark comparisons.
- Recommendations that were not made in a recorded meeting. The letter reports advice; it does not give new advice.
- Tax, legal, or estate conclusions. Reference the conversation, refer to their professional.
- Guarantees, promises of availability, or commitments the advisor didn't make.
- Urgency or scarcity framing of any kind.
- The firm's own metrics, growth, awards, or marketing.

If the advisor asks for any of the above, say which item it is and why it's out, and offer the compliant version of the same intent. "You're on track" becomes "your target is unchanged from last year" — same reassurance, no assurance.

## Structure of the deliverable

- **.docx** by default (read the docx skill first); .pdf on request.
- Header block: draft notice, client name, date, advisor name. Leave the firm name as `[Firm name]` unless the advisor gives it — guessing it from a domain or a meeting title is how a letter goes out with the wrong entity on it.
- Body: the four sections, headings, short paragraphs, no bullet lists longer than four items.
- Footer: a clearly marked placeholder block —

  > `[FIRM DISCLAIMER — replace with your firm's approved language before this letter leaves the building]`

  The disclaimer is deliberately **not remembered between runs**. Firms revise compliance language, and a cached block quietly pasted into next year's letters is a compliance failure with an AI's fingerprints on it. The advisor pastes the current approved text into the .docx, where their own review process can see it.
- No logos, no colour, no letterhead. Firms have their own; a plain draft drops into it.

## Handoff note

After producing the letter, tell the advisor in three short lines: what was redacted and why, anything the record couldn't support that they may want to add themselves, and the reminder that it hasn't been sent and needs their compliance review. Then stop. Don't offer to send it, and don't offer to soften the compliance rules.
